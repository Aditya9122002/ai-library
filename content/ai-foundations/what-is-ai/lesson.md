Artificial Intelligence, or AI, gets used to describe everything from your phone's camera to robots in movies. That's confusing — because "AI" isn't one single thing. It's a broad field, made up of many different techniques, all aimed at getting machines to do things that normally require human intelligence.

Think of "AI" the way you'd think of "sports." Sports isn't one activity — it's a category that includes football, chess, swimming, and archery. Similarly, AI is a category that includes many different approaches: some AI systems recognize your voice, some recommend what to watch next, some generate images from a text description. They're all "AI," but they work in very different ways and were built for very different jobs.

## Narrow AI vs. General AI

Almost every AI system you interact with today falls into one category: **Narrow AI**. Narrow AI is built to do one specific task well — and nothing else. A spam filter is narrow AI: it's excellent at spotting spam emails, but it has no idea how to play chess, recommend a movie, or hold a conversation. Each narrow AI system is a specialist, not a generalist.

**General AI** (sometimes called AGI — Artificial General Intelligence) is different: a hypothetical system that could understand and learn *any* intellectual task a human can, and transfer knowledge between them the way people do. General AI does not exist yet. It's a long-term goal researchers are working toward, not something running on your phone today.

This distinction matters because a lot of hype and confusion about AI comes from mixing these two up. When someone says "AI is going to take over," they're often imagining General AI — but the AI in your life right now is narrow, task-specific, and far more limited than science fiction suggests.

## What Can AI Actually Do?

Even though AI systems are narrow, they're capable of a surprising range of tasks. Most fall into one of these categories:

- **Recognize** — identifying what something is. Your phone unlocking by recognizing your face, or an app identifying a plant from a photo.
- **Predict** — estimating what's likely to happen next. Predicting traffic delays, or estimating how long your food delivery will take.
- **Generate** — creating something new. Writing text, generating an image, or composing music based on a prompt.
- **Recommend** — suggesting what you might want. Deciding what video to show you next, or what product to suggest while shopping.
- **Classify** — sorting things into categories. Marking an email as spam or not spam, or tagging a photo as containing a dog.

Almost every AI product you've used fits into one or more of these five buckets. Once you can spot which one is happening, AI stops feeling mysterious — it's just a system doing one of these five jobs, usually by learning from a large number of examples (we'll get into exactly how in a later chapter).

## Common Myths About AI

A few misunderstandings show up constantly, so let's clear them up early:

**Myth: AI "thinks" like a human.**
AI systems don't understand meaning the way people do. They find patterns in data and use those patterns to produce outputs. It can look like understanding, but under the hood, it's pattern-matching at a massive scale — not reasoning like a human mind.

**Myth: AI is always right.**
AI systems make mistakes — sometimes confidently and convincingly. They're only as good as the data and design behind them, and they don't have real-world judgment to fall back on when something is off.

**Myth: More AI means smarter AI.**
A system built to recognize faces isn't "smarter" than a system that filters spam — they're just built for different, narrow tasks. Capability doesn't stack the way it might in a video game.

**Myth: AI is brand new.**
AI as a field is decades old. What's new is how visible and accessible it's become recently — we'll cover this properly in the next chapter, on the history of AI.

## Why AI Matters Right Now

AI has moved from research labs into everyday products at a scale that hasn't happened before. It's in the apps you already use, the recommendations you see, the way companies operate, and increasingly, the tools people use to write, design, and build things. Understanding what AI actually is — beyond the hype and the fear — is quickly becoming as fundamental as understanding how the internet or smartphones work.
